**Q12:** Fire alarm oversight waits for complaints from affected parties (reactive, cheap). Police patrol actively monitors continuously (proactive, expensive). A welfare bureaucracy might prefer fire alarms because: (1) it's costless—no resources spent monitoring, (2) reveals which violations matter to recipients, (3) provides political cover—only act when constituents complain.

**Q13:** The veto player accepts proposals between positions 20-50. They accept anything closer to their ideal (20) than the status quo (50). Any position from 20 to 50 is closer to 20 than 50 is, so acceptable. Anything above 50 is further from their ideal, so rejected.

**Q14:** The norm collapses suddenly due to critical thresholds. When senior person violates, social cost drops from 5 to 3 units. If 3 units falls below someone's benefit threshold, they defect too, further reducing social cost. This cascade continues—each defection lowers cost for others until norm completely collapses. Threshold effects create sudden rather than gradual change.

**Q15:** This illustrates competing principals problem. IT director receives contradictory demands: COO wants stability (minimal changes) while CIO wants innovation (new systems). Unable to satisfy both, directors face constant criticism and quit. Solution: Clear hierarchical reporting (IT reports only to CIO), OR divide IT into operations (under COO) and development (under CIO).

**Q16:** Voting cycles: Thai beats Pizza (A and C prefer), Pizza beats Burger (A and B prefer), Burger beats Thai (B and C prefer). Using Borda count: assign 3 points for 1st, 2 for 2nd, 1 for 3rd. This violates Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives—ranking between two options depends on third option.

**Q17:** Founders traded flexibility for stability. 90% threshold prevents frequent changes, ensuring constitutional stability and protecting minorities. But makes necessary updates nearly impossible. Wisdom depends on context: wise for fundamental rights, unwise for operational details. Twenty-year gridlock suggests threshold too high.

**Q18:** This creates adverse selection (lemons problem). Information asymmetry causes market failure. Buyers only pay average price (€5500), but seller of €5000 car might withdraw, leaving only worse cars, driving down quality/price further, potentially unraveling entire market.

**Q19:** Universal Domain requires voting system work for ANY preference ordering. Single-peaked excludes preferences like preferring extremes but not moderate positions (e.g., wanting very high OR very low taxes but not medium). These aren't irrational, just non-single-peaked.

**Q20 Model Answer:** Arrow's Impossibility Theorem proves no voting system satisfies all desirable properties simultaneously—we get cycles where A>B>C>A. McKelvey's Chaos shows in multiple dimensions, agenda manipulation can lead anywhere from any starting point.

[Diagram here showing cycling in 2D space]

Institutions respond with constraints. Veto players limit changes from status quo—only proposals making veto player better off pass. This sacrifices majoritarianism but gains stability and minority protection. Agenda setters control what's voted on, preventing chaos while guiding toward their preferences within winset.

Values gained: Stability (no wild cycling), minority protection (veto prevents tyranny), predictability (limited outcomes), coherent governance.

Values lost: Pure majoritarianism (majorities blocked), equality (agenda setters have more power), responsiveness (status quo bias).

These constraints make democracy workable despite mathematical impossibilities, trading theoretical perfection for practical functionality.

**Q21 Model Answer:** Domain 1: Academic Research

- Cooperation problems: Duplication, data hoarding, false results seem to need central planning, mandatory sharing, strict pre-approval.

- Why formal institutions fail: Creativity requires freedom. Central planning kills serendipity. Mandatory sharing removes data collection incentives. Rigid methods prevent innovation.

- Informal mechanisms: Peer review maintains quality through reputation. Citation norms give credit. Conferences share work-in-progress. Plagiarism shame is career-ending.

Domain 2: Online Communities

- Cooperation problems: Trolling, vandalism, misinformation seem to need heavy moderation, identity verification.

- Why formal fails: Rules can't capture context. Heavy moderation stifles discussion. Identity requirements kill participation.

- Informal mechanisms: Community norms (downvoting), reputation systems (karma), volunteer moderators who understand context, social ostracism.

**Q22 Model Answer:** Nash equilibrium: No player wants to deviate unilaterally. Example: [Diagram here - Prisoner's Dilemma matrix] (Defect, Defect) is Nash—neither gains by switching alone. Stability from individual optimization.

Voting equilibrium: No alternative beats current outcome in majority vote. Example: [Diagram here - 1D spatial model with median voter] Median voter's ideal wins—no other point gets majority. Stability from majority preference.

Both identify stable "resting points" predicting outcomes. Both show stability ≠ optimality (Nash can be inefficient, voting can make everyone worse off).

Key differences: Nash focuses on individual strategic choice; voting on collective aggregation. Nash stability from unprofitable unilateral deviation; voting from no majority coalition for change. Nash always exists (in mixed strategies); voting equilibria may not (cycles).

## Part E: Long Answer (15 points - choose 1)

**Q26 Model Answer:**

Using internet governance as example:

- **Anarchy to norms**: Early internet had no rules. Problems emerged (spam, abuse). Users developed netiquette, RFCs, community shaming—organic norms from repeated interaction.

- **Norms to institutions**: As internet scaled, informal norms failed. New users didn't know netiquette. Spam industrialized. Led to ICANN, cybercrime laws, GDPR, Terms of Service—formal institutions necessary.

- **Institutional pathologies**: ICANN became politicized. DMCA enables censorship. Privacy laws created cookie-banner hell. Terms of Service became unaccountable private government. Innovation slowed under regulatory uncertainty.

- **Return to ****"****anarchy****"**: New anarchic spaces deliberately emerged: cryptocurrency (no central control), Tor (avoids surveillance), open source (rejects ownership), federated social media (avoids monopolies). Not old anarchy but conscious choice that ungoverned spaces better serve certain goals.

Shows neither pure anarchy nor complete institutionalization works—different aspects need different approaches simultaneously.

**Q27 Model Answer:**

Paragraph 1: Arrow's Impossibility Theorem is truly insurmountable—it's mathematical proof, not empirical claim. No voting system can simultaneously satisfy universal domain, Pareto efficiency, independence, non-dictatorship, and transitivity. This isn't failure of imagination but logical necessity, like 2+2≠5. Every voting system must sacrifice at least one desirable property.

Paragraph 2: Agenda-setting power manages Arrow's impossibility without solving it. By controlling what gets voted on and in what order, agenda setters prevent infinite cycling while maintaining democratic voting. Trade-off: sacrifice pure equality (agenda setter has disproportionate influence) for actual decisions rather than endless cycles. Congressional committees exemplify this—gatekeeping prevents chaos while enabling governance.

Paragraph 3: Understanding these limits makes me more optimistic about democracy because I have realistic expectations. Rather than being disillusioned when democracy produces "irrational" outcomes, I understand these as mathematical inevitabilities to manage. Seeing how the EU Parliament uses rapporteurs or how ranked-choice voting trades independence for reduced strategic voting shows democracy's "bugs" can be managed as features. We can't perfect democracy, but can consciously choose trade-offs.
