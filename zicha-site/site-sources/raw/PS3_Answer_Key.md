## Learning Objectives and Overview

## Part I: From Cooperation to Coordination - Building Hardin's Insight (45 minutes)

### Teaching Box: Understanding the Constitutional Paradox

Russell Hardin's breakthrough insight: Constitutions appear to solve collective action problems but can't actually enforce themselves. They work not because they force compliance but because they create focal points for coordination. Once everyone expects everyone else to follow constitutional rules, deviation becomes self-punishing through loss of coordination benefits.

### Question 1: Why Prisoner's Dilemmas Need External Enforcement

*In a prisoner's dilemma, both players have dominant strategies that lead to mutual harm. Consider two neighboring towns deciding whether to maintain a shared bridge:*

|  | **Town B** |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | **Maintain** | **Don't Maintain** |
| **Town A** |  |  |
| **Maintain** | (3,3) | (-2,4) |
| **Don't Maintain** | (4,-2) | (-1,-1) |

**Understanding the Payoffs:**

- **Both Maintain (3,3)**: Each town pays 5 units in maintenance costs but gets 8 units of bridge benefit = net 3

- **One Maintains, Other Doesn't (-2,4)**:

- Maintainer: Pays full 5 units cost, gets only partial bridge benefit of 3 units = -2

- Non-maintainer: Pays nothing, gets partial bridge benefit of 4 units = 4

- **Neither Maintains (-1,-1)**: Bridge deteriorates, each loses 1 unit from lack of access

- This creates the prisoner's dilemma: not maintaining is always individually better, but mutual maintenance is collectively optimal

#### [DO] Verify the PD structure:

- Town A's dominant strategy: ____

- Equilibrium outcome: _______

**Answers:**

- Town A's dominant strategy: **Don't Maintain** (4 > 3 when B maintains; -1 > -2 when B doesn't)

- Equilibrium outcome: **Both Don't Maintain (-1,-1)**

#### [WRITE] a) What happens to this bridge without external enforcement? Why can't the towns just agree to maintain it? (2-3 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Without external enforcement, the bridge deteriorates as both towns choose not to maintain it, resulting in the (-1,-1) outcome. Even though mutual maintenance (3,3) would benefit both towns more, each has an individual incentive to free-ride on the other's maintenance efforts. Verbal agreements to cooperate aren't self-enforcing because defection remains profitable regardless of the other town's choice—the very definition of a prisoner's dilemma.

**Common Student Mistake:** Assuming that because cooperation is mutually beneficial, rational actors will achieve it. This confuses collective rationality with individual rationality.

#### [WRITE] b) What would change if a state government could fine towns for not maintaining infrastructure? How does this external enforcement solve the PD? (2-3 sentences):

*Model Answer:* State fines would add a cost to non-maintenance, changing the payoff structure. If fines exceed 4 units (the benefit from free-riding), not maintaining is no longer dominant. With a fine of 5 units, for example, the payoffs for not maintaining become 4-5=-1 when the other maintains, and -1-5=-6 when neither maintains. This transforms the game from a prisoner's dilemma into one where maintenance becomes the dominant strategy, making cooperation self-sustaining even if the state never actually collects a fine.

**Key Insight:** External enforcement doesn't need to be constantly applied—its mere possibility changes the game structure so that cooperation becomes individually rational.

### Question 2: Coordination Games Need No External Enforcement

*Now consider two towns that must coordinate their emergency radio systems. They must use the SAME frequency or communication fails during disasters:*

|  | **Town B** |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | **Frequency A** | **Frequency B** |
| **Town A** |  |  |
| **Frequency A** | (3,3) | (0,0) |
| **Frequency B** | (0,0) | (3,3) |

#### [DO] Identify the Nash equilibria: Either both use A, or both use B

**Answer:** Nash equilibria are **(Freq A, Freq A) and (Freq B, Freq B)**

#### [WRITE] **The Hardin Insight**: Once both towns coordinate on Frequency A, neither wants to switch even without enforcement. Why? What makes this fundamentally different from the bridge problem? (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Once both towns coordinate on Frequency A, neither wants to switch because doing so would destroy all communication ability—their payoff would drop from 3 to 0. This fundamentally differs from the bridge problem where defection always paid. In coordination games, the equilibrium is self-enforcing: unilateral deviation hurts the deviator most. No external enforcement is needed because each player's best response is to match what others are doing. This is why constitutions work—once everyone coordinates on following constitutional rules, deviation means exclusion from the benefits of coordinated society.

**Key Insight:** Constitutions create focal points for coordination. Their power comes not from enforcement but from creating common expectations that become self-fulfilling.

### Question 3A: First-Mover Advantage in Constitutional Choice

*Three groups in a newly independent nation can each propose a constitutional system. Whichever group moves first will establish their preferred system, which then becomes a stable coordination equilibrium (no one wants to deviate alone):*

|  | **Urban Elite (30%)** | **Rural Majority (50%)** | **Military (20%)** | **Total** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Elite Rule** | 9 | 1 | 1 | 11 |
| **Popular Democracy** | 2 | 7 | 2 | 11 |
| **Military State** | 1 | 1 | 9 | 11 |
| **No coordination** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

#### [DO] Analysis:

- If Urban Elite moves first, they establish: _____

- If Rural Majority moves first, they establish: ______

- If Military moves first, they establish: _____

- Once ANY system is established, would anyone deviate unilaterally? _____

**Answers:**

- If Urban Elite moves first: **Elite Rule**

- If Rural Majority moves first: **Popular Democracy**

- If Military moves first: **Military State**

- Would anyone deviate unilaterally? **No** (1 > 0 from chaos)

#### [WRITE] This shows how constitutional moments depend on who controls the transition. Why do all three systems have equal total welfare (11) but radically different distributions? What does this say about the importance of controlling constitutional formation? (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* All three systems yield identical total welfare (11) but with radically different distributions—the controlling group gets 9 while others get only 1. This reveals that constitutional formation is fundamentally about distribution, not efficiency. Whoever controls the transition can lock in their preferred equilibrium, and path dependence makes this choice sticky. Even groups disadvantaged by the system (getting only 1) won't unilaterally defect because coordination on any system beats chaos (1 > 0). This explains why constitutional moments are so contentious—they determine not just rules but who benefits from those rules for generations.

**Note: This problem requires an assumption not stated in the original—a switching cost of approximately 2 units once a system is established. Without this cost, the disadvantaged groups could coordinate to overthrow the first-mover's choice (e.g., Rural Majority and Military coordinating on Popular Democracy to overthrow Elite Rule). The switching cost makes the group that would gain least from changing (Military or Elites, who only get 2 under Popular Democracy) unwilling to pay the cost to switch, preventing the coordination needed to change systems.**

**Common Student Mistake:** Believing the majority will eventually impose their preferred system. Once coordination occurs (and with switching costs), even minority-benefiting equilibria can persist.

**Key Insight:** Constitutional moments are rare opportunities to select among multiple stable equilibria. Control over this selection process matters more than popular support when switching costs exist.

### Question 3B: Coordination Failure vs. Veil of Ignorance

*Now consider a case where the military currently holds power and will impose Military Rule unless civilian groups coordinate on an alternative:*

|  | **Elites (30%)** | **Masses (50%)** | **Military (20%)** | **Total** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Military Rule** | 2 | 2 | 9 | 13 |
| **Elite Democracy** | 8 | 3 | 2 | 13 |
| **Popular Democracy** | 4 | 6 | 3 | 13 |
| **Federal Compromise** | 5 | 5 | 5 | 15 |
| **No coordination** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

#### [DO] Under full information (military holds power):

- Military will impose: _____

- Civilians (Elites + Masses) are: ________

- But Elites prefer: ______ while Masses prefer: _______

- Result: ________

**Answers:**

- Military will impose: **Military Rule**

- Civilians are: **Better off coordinating on alternative**

- But Elites prefer: **Elite Democracy** while Masses prefer: **Popular Democracy**

- Result: **Coordination failure → Military Rule imposed**

#### [DO] Behind veil of ignorance (don't know your group):

- Expected value of Military Rule: (0.3×2) + (0.5×2) + (0.2×9) = ___

- Expected value of Elite Democracy: (0.3×8) + (0.5×3) + (0.2×2) = ___

- Expected value of Popular Democracy: (0.3×4) + (0.5×6) + (0.2×3) = ___

- Expected value of Federal Compromise: (0.3×5) + (0.5×5) + (0.2×5) = ___

**Answers:**

- Military Rule: **3.4**

- Elite Democracy: **4.3**

- Popular Democracy: **4.8**

- Federal Compromise: **5.0**

#### [WRITE] Compare 3A and 3B: In 3A, first-mover wins regardless of efficiency. In 3B, coordination failure leads to military rule despite better alternatives existing. How does the veil of ignorance solve BOTH problems? (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Problem 3A shows how first-mover advantage allows any group to impose their preferred system regardless of efficiency or majority preference. Problem 3B demonstrates how coordination failure among opponents lets inferior outcomes persist—civilians can't unite against military rule because they disagree on alternatives. The veil of ignorance solves BOTH problems by converting distributional conflicts into mutual insurance problems. When people don't know their future position, they evaluate systems by expected value rather than certain benefit, leading all to prefer the balanced Federal Compromise (5.0) over systems that favor particular groups. This transforms zero-sum distributional politics into positive-sum institutional design.

**Key Insight:** Position uncertainty (veil of ignorance) can transform destructive distributional conflicts into constructive constitutional design by aligning individual and collective interests.

## Part II: Constitutional Types and Enforcement Mechanisms (45 minutes)

### Question 4: Sovereign vs. Contracted Constitutions

**Hardin's Framework:**

- **Sovereign constitutions** (nations, UN): No external enforcer. Stability comes from coordination - everyone follows because everyone expects everyone else to follow. Failure means chaos/civil war.

- **Contracted constitutions** (corporations, clubs): Courts can enforce rules. Stability comes from legal remedies, BUT firms still fail through poor decisions or market forces.

*Key difference: Sovereign constitutions can only be replaced through coordination on a new equilibrium. Contracted ones can be enforced by courts but may still collapse if the underlying business fails.*

#### [WRITE] a) Why might the U.S. Constitution (sovereign) be MORE stable than a corporate charter (contracted)? Consider what happens when coordination fails vs. when a lawsuit is filed. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Sovereign constitutions can be more stable than contracted ones precisely because they lack external enforcement. When a sovereign constitution fails, the result is potential civil war or state collapse—catastrophic outcomes that make all parties extremely cautious about deviation. By contrast, corporate charter disputes just mean litigation costs. Additionally, sovereign constitutions coordinate millions of daily behaviors; their collapse would disrupt entire societies. A corporation can fail from bad business decisions even with perfect legal compliance, but a sovereign constitution only fails if coordination collapses. The very absence of external enforcement raises the stakes so high that self-enforcement becomes stronger.

**Common Student Mistake:** Assuming external enforcement always increases stability, missing that high stakes can be more stabilizing than legal remedies.

#### [WRITE] b) The Articles of Confederation (1781-1789) failed as states increasingly ignored federal obligations. Using coordination game logic, explain why states couldn't just "fix" the Articles and needed a completely new Constitution. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* The Articles represented a failing coordination equilibrium where states increasingly expected others to ignore federal obligations, making their own compliance irrational. You cannot incrementally "fix" a coordination failure because each state's willingness to comply depends on expecting others to comply—once that expectation erodes, amendments won't restore it. The Constitutional Convention succeeded by creating an entirely new focal point with different enforcement mechanisms (federal supremacy, direct taxation, federal courts) that reset expectations completely. It wasn't just better rules but a complete re-coordination on a different equilibrium, requiring simultaneous commitment through ratification rather than gradual reform.

**Key Insight:** Failed coordination equilibria require complete replacement, not repair, because expectations are self-reinforcing.

### Question 5: Competing Principals and Agents

*Organizations can structure delegation to create competition that serves the principal's interests.*

**Competing Agents**: Dictators often create overlapping security services that monitor each other. Stalin had the NKVD, GRU, and Party intelligence all reporting separately.

**Competing Principals in Democracy**:

- **Party Competition**: Democrats vs. Republicans both trying to control/influence the same agency

- **Interbranch Competition**: Congress vs. President both claiming authority over agencies

**[Information Problem Framework]:** Consider this scenario: If Stalin had only ONE security agency and they discovered a conspiracy against him, they could:

- Report it honestly (and get rewarded)

- Hide it and join it (and potentially rule)

- Use knowledge of it to blackmail others

Now with THREE agencies: If Agency A hides something, Agency B or C might discover and report it, getting A purged and earning Stalin's favor. This competition for Stalin's approval ensures information flows upward.

**Key prompt:** Why would the NKVD eagerly report on GRU failures? What happens to an agency that fails to report what rivals discover?

#### [WRITE] a) Why would Stalin want three secret police forces instead of one efficient agency? What information problem does this solve? (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Stalin's multiple security agencies solved his fundamental information problem: a monopolistic security agency could hide threats against him or even join conspiracies. With three competing agencies (NKVD, GRU, Party intelligence), each has incentives to report on others' failures to gain Stalin's favor. If Agency A hides a conspiracy, Agencies B or C might discover both the conspiracy AND the cover-up, getting A purged while earning rewards. This competition for Stalin's approval ensures information flows upward—agencies fear rivals will expose any concealment. The inefficiency of triplication is the price for preventing information monopolies that could destroy the dictator.

**Key Insight:** Information monopolies threaten principals more than inefficiency does.

#### [WRITE] b) How does party competition (Democrats vs. Republicans) affect agency behavior? Consider how agencies navigate when control might shift every election. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Party competition creates strategic uncertainty—agencies must satisfy current political masters while hedging against future power shifts. This leads to risk-averse "autopilot" behavior focused on technical tasks both parties support. Agencies avoid initiatives that might anger the next election's winners, develop relationships with both parties' congressional members, and maintain extensive documentation to justify decisions to future overseers. The result is conservative, procedure-focused agencies that prioritize political survival over policy innovation.

#### [WRITE] c) How does interbranch competition (Congress vs. President) differ from party competition? Consider constitutional authority, time horizons, and blame dynamics. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Interbranch competition is constitutionally permanent while party competition is electorally temporary. Congress and President simultaneously claim authority, forcing agencies to navigate between competing legal mandates rather than just political preferences. Congress controls budgets (long-term leverage) while Presidents control appointments (short-term power). This creates a blame-avoidance dynamic where agencies claim they're following congressional statute against presidential pressure or vice versa. Unlike party competition's uncertainty, interbranch competition provides stable strategies for deflecting responsibility. Agencies gain autonomy by playing constitutional principals against each other.

**Key Insight:** Multiple principals can paradoxically increase agent autonomy by providing excuses for selective compliance.

### Question 6: From Roving to Stationary Bandits

*Mancur Olson observed that a **"**roving bandit**"** who steals everything differs from a **"**stationary bandit**"** who limits extraction to maintain the tax base. Some scholars (Munger **&** Munger) argue legitimate government differs from stationary bandits through consent and constitutional process.*

#### [WRITE] a) What makes a roving bandit become stationary? Consider time horizons and repeated interaction. (2-3 sentences):

*Model Answer:* A roving bandit becomes stationary when expected value from repeated extraction exceeds one-time maximal extraction. The key is time horizons—if a bandit expects to control territory long-term, investing in subjects' productive capacity (through limited extraction and providing security) yields higher total returns than pillaging. Repeated interaction also creates reputational incentives: subjects hide wealth from known excessive extractors but produce more under predictable taxation.

#### [WRITE] b) As positive (not normative) political theory: How do a dictator who seized power and one elected through constitutional process differ in their incentives? Does "legitimacy" necessarily affect behavior? (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* From a positive perspective, the method of gaining power affects incentives through different maintenance costs. A coup leader must continuously pay off the military coalition that brought them to power, requiring higher extraction rates. A constitutionally elected leader who becomes dictatorial faces constraints from institutional remnants and needs less resources for repression since they have some legitimacy. "Legitimacy" affects behavior only by reducing the cost of maintaining control—legitimate rulers need fewer resources for repression, allowing more productive investment. The difference is structural (different power maintenance costs) not moral.

**Key Insight:** Legitimacy matters for behavior only through its effect on the costs of maintaining power.

The cooling-off period might intensify corruption—inspectors could be "purchased" through future job promises made before they even start government service. Parliamentary control might make environmental policy too responsive to electoral cycles.

**Key Insight:** Every accountability mechanism creates new gaming opportunities—the goal is channeling strategic behavior in less harmful directions.

### Question 8: Types of Monitoring - Fire Alarms vs. Police Patrols

*Principals monitor agents through two methods:*

- **Police Patrols**: Active, continuous monitoring (costly but comprehensive)

- **Fire Alarms**: Wait for complaints from affected parties (cheap but incomplete)

*Consider Congress overseeing environmental regulation:*

| **Monitoring Type** | **Cost** | **Coverage** | **Information Quality** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Police Patrol | High | Systematic | Detailed | Regular EPA audits |
| Fire Alarm | Low | Sporadic | May miss problems | Wait for citizen complaints |

#### [WRITE] a) Why might Congress prefer fire alarms even if they miss many violations? Consider the costs and political benefits. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Congress prefers fire alarms because they're cost-free to maintain and provide political benefits. Congress doesn't spend resources on monitoring but can claim credit for responding when constituents complain. Fire alarms also provide political cover—Congress acts only when voters demand action, avoiding blame for "regulatory overreach." Additionally, fire alarms reveal which violations actually matter to voters, allowing Congress to focus on politically salient issues rather than technical compliance.

#### [WRITE] b) When would police patrols be essential despite the cost? Give a specific regulatory example and explain why fire alarms would fail. (3-4 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Police patrols are essential for nuclear power plant safety where fire alarms would catastrophically fail. By the time citizens could observe and report safety violations (radiation leakage), irreversible harm would have occurred. The technical complexity means citizens can't evaluate safety compliance even with full access. Only continuous, expert monitoring can detect problems like metal fatigue or improper waste handling before they become disasters.

**Key Insight:** Fire alarms work for visible, non-catastrophic violations but fail for complex, hidden, or irreversible problems.

*Model Answer:* The Maximum Information system identifies and fixes problems much faster because workers report issues immediately without fear. Effective organizations deliberately limit punishment power (like Toyota's "no-blame" culture) to maximize information flow.

**Key Insight:** Power and information are substitutes, not complements—the pursuit of absolute control creates absolute blindness.

### Question 10: Sovereign-Contracted Coordination - The COVID Vaccine Case

*The COVID-19 pandemic required unprecedented coordination between sovereign constitutions (governments) and contracted constitutions (pharmaceutical companies) to develop and distribute vaccines.*

**Normal times - Interests and behaviors:**

- Pharmaceutical companies: Want approval but fear lawsuits from adverse events

- Regulators: Fear approving dangerous drugs more than delaying good ones

- Different companies: Compete by exposing rivals' problems to gain market share

- Different countries: Maintain independent standards, skeptical of others' approvals

**Crisis times - Interests and behaviors:**

- Pharmaceutical companies: Want first-mover advantage, received liability protection

- Regulators: Fear blame for delays more than for approval errors

- Different companies: All want vaccine market to succeed, avoid undermining confidence

- Different countries: Want to show competence, avoid being last to approve

#### [DO] Track the incentive changes step-by-step:

**Step 1 - Regulatory incentives:**

- Before crisis: Regulators feared [approving a drug that causes harm / Thalidomide-type disaster] more than [delaying a beneficial drug]

- During crisis: Regulators feared [being blamed for vaccine delays causing deaths] more than [rare side effects]

**Step 2 - Company competition:**

- Normally: Competition creates [incentive to expose rival problems / "our drug is safer than theirs"]

- During COVID: Competition shifted to [mutual support / "vaccines are safe and effective"]

- Why? Because [undermining any vaccine would destroy confidence in all vaccines]

**Step 3 - Key change:** Complete this sentence: "The crisis converted normally competing actors into ________ because ________"

**Answer:** "The crisis converted normally competing actors into **aligned supporters** because **shared existential threat to entire vaccine market**"

#### [WRITE] Analysis:

**a) Competition dynamics**: Compare how pharmaceutical companies interacted with each other before vs. during the crisis. What changed about their competitive behavior and why? Consider market stakes, reputational interdependence, and government protections. (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Before COVID, pharmaceutical companies competed by emphasizing superior safety profiles—"our drug has fewer side effects than Drug X." During COVID, this reversed completely: highlighting any vaccine's risks would undermine public confidence in ALL vaccines, destroying the entire market including their own products. Companies shifted from competitive differentiation to collective reputation management. Government liability protection and guaranteed purchases removed normal competitive pressures. The shared need for vaccine acceptance created unprecedented industry solidarity—Pfizer would defend Moderna's safety and vice versa.

**b) Regulatory independence**: Multiple national regulators normally provide independent checks on each other's decisions. What happened to this independence during COVID? Why might regulators begin deferring to each other's approvals? (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Multiple national regulators normally provide independent validation—FDA approval doesn't guarantee EMA approval, creating redundant safety checks. During COVID, regulators faced unprecedented pressure to avoid being the "blocker" nation delaying vaccines while others proceeded. Once the UK approved first, other regulators faced political pressure to follow or explain why British citizens could have vaccines but theirs couldn't. This created an information cascade where early approvals influenced later ones, undermining independent evaluation. Regulators began sharing data and coordinating decisions, reducing the independence that normally provides diverse safety perspectives.

**c) Safety mechanisms**: Given the incentive changes you identified, what happened to the normal safety checks in the system? Who, if anyone, maintained incentives to be skeptical? (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* The normal safety checks—competitive companies exposing rivals' problems, independent regulators providing diverse evaluations, and liability concerns forcing caution—all aligned toward rapid approval. The only actors maintaining skepticism incentives were those outside the mainstream system: alternative medicine advocates (but they lacked credibility), trial lawyers (but liability protections neutralized them), and some academic researchers (but they faced enormous professional pressure). Traditional media, normally skeptical of pharmaceutical claims, instead became vaccine advocates to combat "misinformation." The system lost its normal "devil's advocates" who profit from finding problems.

**d) Design lesson**: What does this case teach about maintaining beneficial competition between agents during crises? How can systems preserve diverse incentives when political pressure pushes toward alignment? (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* This case reveals how crises can dangerously align normally competing interests, eliminating beneficial friction. To preserve diverse incentives during crises, systems should institutionalize "red teams" specifically rewarded for skepticism, similar to military adversarial exercises. Create guaranteed funding for independent verification that activates automatically during emergency authorizations. Designate specific actors whose mandate explicitly includes skepticism even during crises, protecting them from political pressure. Systems should hardwire contrarian voices that can't be swept away by crisis consensus.

**Key Insight:** Crises that align normally competing interests eliminate crucial system checks, requiring deliberate preservation of skeptical voices.

**a) Niskanen's insight**: Why do agencies request the maximum budget Congress might approve rather than what they actually need? Consider the agency's objective function and information asymmetry. How does this differ from a private firm's behavior? (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Agencies request maximum budgets Congress might approve rather than actual needs because they're budget-maximizers, not profit-maximizers. Programs create concentrated beneficiaries who lobby intensely while costs diffuse across taxpayers who barely notice. **c) Comparative solutions**: Which oversight mechanism best controls bureaucratic growth? Why do sunset provisions often fail despite seeming logical? Consider concentrated benefits vs. diffuse costs. (4-5 sentences):

*Model Answer:* Competing agencies work best because they have incentives to expose each other's inefficiencies to capture budget share. Congressional hearings fail because agencies control the information and beneficiaries provide supportive testimony. Private contracting creates different pathologies—contractors lobby for work and may be more efficient at extraction than bureaucrats.

*Model Answer:* Create "efficiency dividends" where agencies keep 50% of cost savings for three years to spend on employee bonuses or new initiatives, making efficiency profitable. Create independent cost assessment offices that audit agency budgets against private sector benchmarks. New pathologies: Agencies might cut quality rather than waste to claim "efficiency." Competing bureaus duplicate overhead. Automatic shrinking might eliminate valuable programs lacking vocal constituencies. The fundamental tension: we want agencies efficient but responsive, frugal but effective.

**Key Insight:** Bureaucratic growth is structural, not accidental—agencies lack competition and profit motive in an environment where budget equals success.
