## INTRODUCTION

Governing our own lives, our organizations, and our societies requires decision-making. We often call these decisions—either formally or colloquially—personal, organizational, or government "policies." The policy of a government might be to invade a Middle Eastern oil state, the policy of a group of LUC students might be to go out for Indian food on Sunday night, or the family's method for determining where to go on holiday, and my personal policy might be to avoid apple juice at all costs (nasty preservatives in there!). Of course, many 'policies' themselves define or alter the decision-making process for individuals. Consider, for instance, when a government makes a policy that creates new regulations for a particular industry requiring consultation with environmental assessors. Those new regulations define the decision-making process. Decision-making processes are **central** to questions of governance.

This course provides you with a unified analytical framework for understanding decision-making processes across all scales—from individual choices to global governance. We will build this framework systematically, moving from individual decision-making through decentralized market processes to centralized hierarchical structures and finally to collective choice mechanisms. Along the way, we develop powerful "bookshelf models"—transferable analytical tools including principal-agent theory, spatial models of choice, social choice theory, and game theory—that you can apply throughout your academic and professional life.

But this course operates on multiple levels simultaneously. While we are learning positive political economy and building technical skills, we are also developing a complete analytical framework for understanding how decisions get made in any context. Most importantly, we are cultivating critical reflection on both the tremendous power and the inherent limitations of this analytical approach. In a liberal arts context, this perspective complements rather than competes with other ways of understanding the world—historical, cultural, normative, and interpretive approaches all remain essential.

Contemporary societies involve public, private, and international interactions operating in an increasingly complex environment that structures decisions. By providing you with rigorous analytical tools and perspectives for evaluating both decentralized 'free-market' contexts and more deeply hierarchical and structured contexts, we aim to simplify the complexity such that policy choices that might have seemed puzzling before now appear clear. Or, if not absolutely clear, you will have an idea of where you might want to start when thinking about them.

More importantly, from the perspective of general citizenship, understanding and thinking deeply about decision-making processes at the most fundamental level is essential to understanding the limits and promise of collective organization, and routinely lies at the foundation of most policy debates. But perhaps most importantly, doing so may also suggest ways in which existing processes can be improved. Understanding how we make decisions and what makes for bad decisions is the first and most important step for correction and control.

While the focus will be on decision-making in an organizational context and on policy-making in the public sector, substantial attention will also be paid to assumptions of individual decision-making processes that underpin different theories of collective decision-making processes. Discussions will directly address the application of our understanding of the policy process to businesses, non-governmental organizations, governmental, and international organizations. However, by and large, readings will be more theoretical in nature. This is by design. One of my core goals of the course is to exercise your ability to argue about abstract arguments with great specificity and application to particulars. Most are good about unrigorous abstract conversations, and most can hold their own talking about cases. The real art is recognizing that one always interprets the world through abstractions and that rigorously and carefully evaluating those abstractions as we might a case or on a case, such that you can garner new insights and develop new questions (oh, yes… and win arguments).

## LEARNING OUTCOMES

Upon successful completion of this course, students will:

- **Master transferable analytical models** of decision-making processes across diverse contexts—developing "bookshelf models" (principal-agent theory, spatial choice models, social choice theory, game theory) that can be applied throughout academic and professional life.

- **Apply abstract analytical modeling and reasoning techniques** to concrete cases or issues in decision-making explicitly relevant to global challenges, demonstrating the ability to move fluidly between theory and application.

- **Demonstrate sophisticated understanding** of the nature of complexity and challenges involved in governing collectives of individuals—and develop more nuanced critiques of decision-makers' perceived failings based on structural constraints.

- **Develop their own institutional framework** for how collective decision-making works and why it matters, enabling them to revise and refine this framework throughout further courses and life experience.

- **Critically evaluate** both the power and limitations of positive political economy approaches, understanding how this analytical perspective complements other modes of inquiry in a liberal arts context.

## COURSE PEDAGOGY

This course invites you into four great conversations that have shaped human understanding of decision-making, each weaving together with the others to create a rich intellectual tapestry. The first conversation is about **positive political economy**—learning to analyze decision-making with scientific rigor rather than just offering opinions about how things should work. The second conversation introduces you to **the scholarly tradition and practice of model-building**—learning to think with powerful analytical frameworks that become part of your permanent intellectual equipment, applicable to everything from family dinner decisions to international negotiations. The third conversation constructs a **comprehensive architecture** of decision-making that spans from individual psychology to global governance, showing how choice mechanisms work at every scale. The fourth conversation—perhaps the most important—develops your **interpretive sophistication**: the wisdom to know when this analytical lens reveals essential truths and when other perspectives are needed to complete the picture.

Like voices in a complex musical composition, these four conversations interweave throughout every session, each enriching and challenging the others as we build toward a unified understanding of how decisions shape our world. By course's end, you will have become a 'model thinker'—someone who can not only join these existing conversations but begin to compose your own analytical music, adapting and creating new approaches as you encounter novel challenges. This is not the end of your intellectual story, but rather your initiation into a lifelong capacity to think systematically about the complex decision-making processes that govern human affairs.

True to the conversational nature of these four intellectual streams, our classroom becomes a **community of inquiry** where we gather around decision-making as co-investigators. Rather than transferring knowledge from instructor to student, we approach these texts and ideas as living materials that reveal new insights when examined together. I serve as **guide and fellow learner**—helping pose productive questions, surface assumptions, and connect insights across our explorations.

Our sessions blend focused presentation with **collaborative investigation**. Some days require structured guidance to establish shared understanding; others involve deep collective analysis where your questions drive discovery. This demands **active intellectual citizenship**—not just responding to questions, but bringing your own wondering, connections, and challenges to the material.

This approach requires non-passive, deeply engaged participation. This course is demanding—the most demanding I teach—but historically among the most rewarding for those who commit fully. Spend the full 13 hours a week outside class, keeping a reading notebook that records **your own thinking**—questions, connections, moments of confusion or insight to bring to our community.

Navigating four conversations simultaneously requires trust. Students typically "click" around weeks 5–6 as components crystallize into a coherent framework. The intentional front-loading builds complexity gradually so later applications feel natural and powerful.

**This course is about taking responsibility for your learning while contributing to our collective understanding of how decision-making processes shape our world.**

**Weekly Guide:** We begin by exploring the core idea that decision processes are best understood through models—formal, abstract representations that help us make sense of real organizational and policy puzzles, and the general necessity of it in the social sciences, and arguably any social thought at all. ### Week 2: Micro-Foundations—From Homo Economicus to Psychology

**Weekly Guide:** We move from the abstract value of models to the nuts and bolts: What assumptions do we make about individuals making choices? To build a model of social outcomes in a methodologically individualist way, where we build up from individual actors, we are going to have to have some idea of how we think people… well… are. This week contrasts the rational actor model with critiques from psychology and behavioral economics, showing how models adapt to the realities of human cognition but also to force us to ask serious questions about how much specificity is necessary or even desirable for thinking about how different decision-making situations work out?

#### Session 1: Rational Choice Theory

**Session Guide:** We dissect rational choice theory, seeing how it frames decisions in idealized, logical terms. Pay attention to the readings on rationality's strengths and where the model starts to fray at the edges.

### Week 3: Decentralized Decision—Markets, Anarchy, and Their Limits

**Weekly Guide:** It's obvious from last week that individual decision-making needs to be evaluated in contexts in order to even know how to think about individual decision-making. Moreover, we tend to be interested in decision-making processes beyond mere individuals disconnected from context. We are interested in social decision-making outcomes. So to start building a model of collective decision-making we start with the simplest lowest assumption one possible: Anarchy. A system with no rules, and just unbounded individual decision-making bouncing off each other. We move from there to only slightly more limited market-style decentralized systems which operate with only a few rules and which allow for no explicit centralized decision-making.

Now we ask: How do group decisions happen when no one is "in charge?" Readings trace the promise and perils of decentralized systems (markets/anarchy), leading to a calculus of when rules or authority become necessary.

#### Session 1: Markets and Spontaneous Order

**Session Guide:** Focuses on the power of markets and spontaneous order, showcasing Hayek and the logic of decentralized knowledge. Ask: Why are some societies drawn to "the market" as a solution—and what are its limits?

## Week 1 Baseline Assignment: Your Decision-Making Framework

**Due:** End of Week 1
**Length:** 500–750 words
**Purpose:** To establish your starting point for reflection at course's end

### The Task

Before we dive into formal theories and models, we want to capture how you currently think about decision-making processes. This baseline will serve as a point of comparison for your final reflection essay, helping you see how your thinking has evolved.

### The Prompt

Choose **one concrete decision-making scenario** from the list below (or propose your own with instructor approval). Write a thoughtful analysis that explains how you think this decision gets made in reality—what drives the process, what matters, what doesn't, and why things turn out the way they do.

**Scenario Options:**

- Your student government deciding how to allocate a €10,000 budget for campus activities
- A family of five choosing where to go on vacation when everyone has different preferences
- The Netherlands deciding whether to implement a national carbon tax
- A tech startup's founding team deciding whether to accept venture capital funding
- Your friend group choosing which restaurant to go to when dietary restrictions and budgets vary
- Or propose your own comparable scenario

Tell us **your story** of how this decision-making process works. What's your explanation? What's your theory? Use whatever framework feels natural to you—there are no right or wrong approaches.

### What We're Looking For

We want to see **your authentic thinking**—not what you think we want to hear. Use your intuition, experience, and whatever makes sense to you. The goal is to capture your genuine starting point so we can trace your intellectual journey throughout the course. It isn't graded at all other than being required in order to complete the final assignment which will be graded. So, this assignment will be returned to you at the end of the course to inform your final reflection essay.
