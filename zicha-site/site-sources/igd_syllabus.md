# INTRODUCTION

Global challenges are nearly all, at some level, issues of governance and development. Why are some countries poor while others are wealthy? Why do well-established, seemingly well-functioning countries not tackle serious challenges like climate change more aggressively? Why are human rights enforced by governments in some countries but not in others? Why are some diverse communities peaceful and cooperative, while others are divisive and violent?

This course is the foundational course for the Governance, Economics, and Development major. It is also quite challenging. Its goal is to introduce you to institutional analysis in the context of scientifically studying — and designing solutions for — the problems of governance and development. To some degree, and often to a rather large degree, all of the courses in the GED major draw on the concepts and methods of analysis outlined here. In short, this course is designed to form a foundation for a particular way of thinking. Rather than discuss what we think *should* happen in the pursuit of prosperity, or study specific solutions for addressing global challenges, this course focuses on developing a way of thinking about them.

We accomplish this by studying what has become one of — if not *the* — seminal texts of the new institutional interdisciplinary programme: Douglass North's *Institutions, Institutional Change, and Economic Performance*. Nearly all the fundamental concepts, models, and purposes of analysis for enhancing prosperity within this framework are brought together in this book, and its content serves as a vehicle for introducing and synthesising the subjects pursued in the policy science, development, comparative politics, and economics tracks at the College. But the course carries a more general message, and students who complete it are of value to anyone in the College.

Institutions, defined at a basic level, are fundamental to understanding social processes: they constrain and provide opportunities for individuals to realise their goals. They come in many forms — the formal rules and structures of law, informal social norms, and (depending on your philosophical position on the definition of an institution) even the moral values in your own head. How these change, and how they structure behaviour, is part and parcel of the key questions of how we learn to make better decisions, control the problems we wish to ameliorate, and understand or guide how societies change.

Governance and development are two dimensions of a similar question: how is society organised, how does that organisation change over time, and how might we extend our willful control over those processes through intervention? The first is a question of passive understanding and science; the second, of agency and engineering. Theory versus practice, you might say. Seen this way, institutional analysis also becomes a framework for understanding a fundamental difference between progressives, who see formal institutional change as a key tool for improving social outcomes, and conservatives, who put more weight on informal institutions accumulated over time, such as cultural norms and received hierarchies. The framework forces us to look at our own views on this axis in a new light.

Questions of governance are about how to solve or address individual tasks: how do we, as citizens, control the state? How does the state administer social welfare policy? Questions of development are about how things change over time so as to be on generally positive trajectories — or negative ones — relative to our goals and values, whatever they may be. Many questions of governance are asked by development scholars in the so-called 'developing' world or 'Global South' when they ask how aid can be distributed more effectively or with greater accountability. Likewise, classic scholars of governance often ask questions of development when they ask why there is such an underdeveloped welfare state in the United States. It can be confusing to separate these questions by field, or even by title. By working through them with an institutional framework, we gain clearer insight into how to define, separate, and understand the difference between them.

# LEARNING OBJECTIVES

By the conclusion of the course, students will be able to:

- **Define** the key concepts of the new institutional framework for studying social processes and change.
- **Differentiate** the processes of governance and development in terms of these concepts.
- **Demonstrate familiarity** with basic methodological issues in evaluating institutional analyses of governance and development questions.
- **Understand and apply** the preliminaries of 'model thinking' — abstracting reality into a coherent logical theoretical construct, and a case example.

The primary mode of instruction — lecture versus more seminar-oriented — varies from instructor to instructor. For this section, a mix of seminar dominated by the instructor (think of me as a hyper-participative discussion leader), mini-lecture, and classroom activity is used to complement, cohere, and more deeply communicate core course content. Participation and attendance are therefore important.

There is also a sizeable role — even a need — for self-study and organisation. The reading per session is almost never long, but North is *dense*. Each chapter presents a great deal to discuss, many new concepts, things you will need to look up, and things you will not understand on your own first reading. *Get together, teach each other, and take initiative.* You will come back with far better questions for discussion and, usually, more enthusiasm, because you will have had arguments about the interpretation of North and will be motivated to resolve them in class. So, as in a traditional university course, the expectation and encouragement of self-study and discussion groups is itself a teaching method — albeit one that is not graded directly.

# MEETINGS AND FLOW

The week has two sessions with distinct functions.

**Session 1 — North (Mondays, 17:15–19:00).** The first session of the week works through the assigned chapters of North. In weeks 2 through 6, a **quiz** is given as soon as class begins and lasts no longer than 15 minutes. Any minute you are late is a minute you do not have for the quiz, so be on time. The remainder of the session is seminar discussion of the chapters.

**Session 2 — Application (Thursdays, 13:15–15:00).** The second session extends or applies the material from North to the week's applied readings. In weeks 1–3 and 5–7 the session normally opens with a **pop reflection**: 10–15 minutes, handwritten, half a side of A4. As with the quizzes, any minute you are late is a minute you do not have to write. The remainder of the session is seminar discussion, led from the front. In week 4, the second session is given over to the midterm essay and there is no reflection.

Note that both sessions of the week therefore open with something written. This is deliberate: the quiz checks that you have read North closely, and the letter asks what you made of the week as a whole. Neither rewards cramming in the corridor.

Throughout the course you should make use of the course's online space to ask questions about the readings. One lesson of the Covid period of online teaching was that this is more efficient than raising basic clarification questions in class: you get better answers, you get them sooner, everyone gets them, and everyone can review them later. Please make the thrust of your question clear in the title of your post.

## *Completion requirement*

**Every assessment component must be submitted or attended in order to pass this course**, even where what you produce is incomplete. An unfinished essay handed in is a grade; an essay not handed in is a fail. A student who skips a component fails the course outright, whatever weighted average the remaining work would have earned.

This bites hardest on the midterm essay, the synthetic reflection essay, and the final individual discussion, which are the discrete deliverables. The dropped-lowest allowance on the quizzes and the letters is built into how those components are scored and is unaffected by this rule.

## *Participation — 10%*

Participation is essential because this is, essentially, a seminar. Quality participation and good study habits *require* that you complete the reading. Discussion may flow from questions asked by students or by the instructor. Participation is assessed on the quality of your contributions and, at a minimum, on your preparedness. Coming unprepared is not tolerated: it is disrespectful to classmates whose participation you are using to summarise readings you have not done.

If it becomes apparent that you cannot communicate basic information about the readings, you lose 25% of your participation grade. After four such instances you receive a 0 (F) for participation.

You should aim to participate actively in at least **ten** class discussions. Participation means contributing an original thought or a stimulating question. Participation for a given day is marked on this scale:

| **Mark** | **Meaning** |
|---|---|
| 0 | Did not participate on the material under discussion |
| 0.5 | Participated but contributed nothing original (merely summarised another's point, or spoke only about how the class was being conducted rather than its content) |
| 1 | Made an original contribution |
| 1.5 | Was a repeated and enthusiastic participant |

A 1 is required for full credit on a given day; the extra half point is extra credit. Participation is therefore an *objective assessment* (see Grading policy).

*Note:* Course activities, games, and experiments — but most importantly asking questions and participating in discussion online — may earn bonus points toward your participation grade outside seminar. Being a good citizen counts. They are bonus points because their allocation is not always strictly 'fair'; in some games, for instance, your randomly drawn starting position may be disadvantageous.

## *Weekly in-class quizzes — 20%*

A quiz is given at the start of the first session in each of weeks 2 through 6 (Mondays 2, 9, 16, 23 and 30 November), lasting no more than 15 minutes. Quizzes evaluate how carefully you have read the assigned chapters of North for key concepts and, in usually at least one item per quiz, ask you to integrate those concepts in a short written answer. Your lowest quiz grade is dropped, including a quiz missed through absence. Quizzes are an *objective assessment*.

## *Weekly in-class pop reflections — 25%*

Six times during the block — in the **second session** of weeks 1–3 and 5–7 (Thursdays 29 October, 5, 12 and 26 November, 3 and 10 December) — you will write a short reflection in class, normally at the start of the session. You have 10 to 15 minutes and half a side of A4, handwritten.

Think of these as **letters**. The prompt will either be general — what were you thinking *across* this week's readings? — or a particular concern of mine arising from them. What I am asking for is not a summary, and not an answer to a test question. It is the integrative thought you had while reading: the connection you noticed between two texts, the tension you could not resolve, the objection you wanted to raise, the case from outside the course that an argument reminded you of.

The purpose is twofold. First, to give you a reason to read *actively* rather than passively — to read with a pen in hand, and with the expectation that you will have to say something of your own about what you have read. Second, to give you repeated, low-cost practice at expressing a synthetic, creative, or critical thought concisely and clearly. Half a page is not much room. That constraint is the point.

I will read and respond to every letter, every week. You will get back a reply to the idea itself — whether it holds up, where it might go, what it is missing — along with comment on how you expressed it. This is the most direct written exchange the two of us will have during the block, and it is where most of the individual feedback in this course lives. Treat it as correspondence rather than as submission.

Each letter is worth 5% of your final grade. Your lowest is dropped, including one missed through absence. Because they are written in class, there is no make-up: the dropped grade is the accommodation. Reflections are a *subjective assessment*, graded A–F. Your grade comes back on the reply itself, so you are never more than a week away from knowing where you stand and what would make the next letter better. There is no rubric, deliberately: a grid would tell you how to score rather than how to think, and the reply does the work a grid would only pretend to do.

**Models and recognition.** Each week I may post the three strongest letters to the course's online space so that everyone can see what a good one looks like. Models teach this better than instructions do: half a page of someone else's real thinking will show you more about what the exercise wants than any description I could write. Letters are posted under the author's name unless you would rather they were not — tell me and I will post yours anonymously or leave it out. The author of the week's strongest letter also earns a bonus on their **midterm essay** grade.

## *In-class midterm essay — 20%*

A one-hour written assignment sat in class in week 4, session 2 — **Thursday 19 November, 13:15**. The prompt is distributed at the beginning of the session and time expires 60 minutes later. It evaluates your understanding of the core concepts of the course against a well-defined prompt, and you will receive elaborated written feedback. Students entitled to extra time, or needing to sit it at another time, should arrange this with the instructor in advance.

## *Synthetic reflection essay — 15%*

A take-home essay of no more than 1,500 words, with two connected tasks.

First, you evaluate what you have learned and how it has affected your thinking — an account of your own intellectual development in light of the material we have covered.

Second, you **ground that reflection in a case** — some governance or development situation you find interesting, looked at through the concepts of the course. This is not a research paper and does not need to be exhaustive. It is enough to show the framework doing real work on something concrete: what it brings into view, and what you would want to know next.

The case need not be impressive. The governance of your student association, or the development of your campus community, will serve as well as an Ebola outbreak or the growth of the European welfare state.

Of the 15%, roughly a third rests on the case and the remainder on the reflection. The case is also what sets up your final discussion, so choose one you will want to talk about.

The prompt is distributed in week 7 (Thursday 10 December) and the essay is due on **Sunday 13 December, 23:59** — the Sunday that begins reading week. This is a *subjective assessment*, graded A–F.

## *Final individual discussion — 10%*

During **reading week (Monday 14 – Friday 18 December)**, after your essay is in, you will meet me individually to discuss it — principally the case application, which is what the conversation is built around.

This is a conversation, not a defence. I will ask you about the choices you made: why this case, what the institutional framing brought into view, where the account you built strains against the evidence, what you would do differently with more room. You should be ready to think aloud rather than to recite.

It is also where the substantive feedback on your essay is delivered. Written comments on a final essay arrive after the course has ended and are read once, if at all. A conversation lets you ask the follow-up question, and lets me find the understanding in cases where the prose did not quite carry it.

This 10% is a grade for the discussion in its own right, separate from the grade for the essay it responds to. A thin essay discussed well earns the credit; a strong essay its author cannot talk about does not.

# CLASS POLICIES

## *Behaviour*

I strive to maintain a friendly yet professional climate in my classroom. One of the habits of mind a liberal arts education is meant to form is a love of learning, discourse, and debate about ideas. I promise always to treat *you* with respect, even if I occasionally treat *an idea* with less regard. I ask the same of you. If you disagree with some aspect of how the course is being run, please speak to me after class. Behaviour that disrupts discussion or lecture will result in the offender being asked to leave the session, a zero for participation that day, and an appointment for the two of us with the Dean.

I also want us to have fun, and to be a community. Please use the course's online space to meet, to contact me, and to share things with each other.

## *Attendance*

LUC's recommended attendance policy permits students to miss two classes, and that policy is enforced here. Students who will miss more than two classes must document extenuating circumstances in order to be granted make-up assignments.

If you miss a class for a reason other than an extenuating circumstance, you receive a one-point deduction to your participation grade unless you submit a 2–3 page integrative essay summarising and integrating the readings for the missed session with those of the sessions immediately preceding it. The goal is to demonstrate that you have spent extra time with the material and made an effort to compensate for the time missed in class. It is graded pass/fail and is due one week after the missed session.

*Note:* a good deal of material is delivered during sessions, as some of these readings prove challenging. If you miss class, get together with classmates and review what happened.

## *Late work*

*Late work is usually not accepted.* Extensions are not given for essays or take-home assessments for nearly any reason. To qualify for an extension you must be able to document either that you were subject to extenuating circumstances for a substantial period before the due date — not merely immediately before it — or that you had made sufficient progress on the assignment to demonstrate a reasonable attempt to plan ahead. It is therefore advisable to start your reading and your drafting early.
